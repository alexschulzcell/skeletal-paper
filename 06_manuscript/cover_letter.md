Dear Editors,

We submit for your consideration as a **Report** the manuscript "How widely a gene acts across
the skeleton predicts how widely its mutations cause disease."

**What was known.** Two findings in this journal's own territory contradict each other. Mendelian
disease genes are enriched at the loci of common-variant associations, which implies that rare and
common variation in the same gene converge on related biology. Yet when the comparison is made
across the whole phenome, the number of traits a gene affects in genome-wide association studies
does not predict the number of phenotypes its rare variants cause; the two measures of pleiotropy
are uncorrelated. There has been no resolution, and the null result has been read as evidence that
the convergence is weaker than the enrichment literature suggests.

**The conceptual advance.** We show that the correspondence is real and that it is anatomically
local, which is why a phenome-wide count destroys it. A phenome-wide comparison treats bone
density, serum urate and lung function as interchangeable units of pleiotropy, and counts ontology
terms drawn from every organ on the other side. We instead measured breadth on both sides with the
same anatomy, inside one organ system. Bone mineral density is available at six skeletal sites
that are named after body parts rather than machine settings, and the Human Phenotype Ontology
names the same parts on the disease side. Each gene then carries one number — at how many of the
six sites does it act — and that number predicts whether its mutations cause Mendelian skeletal
disease, in three independent curations, and how much of the body that disease involves.

The result that closes the argument is a negative one we designed in advance. We rebuilt the
entire construction on seven subcortical brain volumes. Skeletal breadth predicts skeletal
phenotype breadth; brain breadth predicts nothing, in either organ. Breadth is therefore not a
proxy for general gene importance, and the reason the brain gives no answer is visible in the
data: its measurement sites share too little genetic architecture for a shared layer to exist at
all. That is the published phenome-wide null result seen from the inside, and it tells the field
where the rare-common correspondence can be found and where it will remain invisible.

**Why it matters to a broad readership.** The same property spans two clinical ends that are
usually studied by different communities. Genes acting across the whole skeleton carry the
childhood dysplasias and, independently, the fracture risk of old age; genes acting at a single
site carry neither. The finding replicates in GEFOS, which shares no participants with UK Biobank,
and survives entry alongside all six site-level statistics from which breadth is derived. For
variant interpretation it supplies a prior that costs nothing to apply and can be read from tables
that already exist.

We have stated the limits in the manuscript rather than leaving them to review. Breadth is a
property of a gene, not a gene list: redefining the layer from scratch in the replication cohort
yields lists that overlap by 19%, so we report breadth as a continuous variable throughout. Within
the layer, fracture signal and Mendelian disease are not associated with each other, so the
defensible claim is the same layer, not the same genes. We report the p-value floor of the
gene-level method and the sensitivity of every threshold we chose; the sweeps are given in full in
the supplemental information rather than summarized.

All analyses use publicly available summary statistics; there are no restrictions on data or
materials. The code that reproduces every number and every figure is public, and a self-test in
the repository checks each numerical claim in the manuscript against the table it came from.

The manuscript is not under consideration elsewhere, and all authors have approved the submission.
