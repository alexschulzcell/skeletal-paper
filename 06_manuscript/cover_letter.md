Dear Editors,

Two findings that this journal has helped establish contradict each other, and nobody has
reconciled them. Mendelian disease genes are enriched at the loci of common-variant associations,
which says that rare and common variation in the same gene converge on the same biology. Yet when
the comparison is made across the whole phenome, the number of traits a gene affects in GWAS does
not predict the number of phenotypes its rare variants cause. The two measures of pleiotropy are
uncorrelated, and the field has read that null as evidence that the convergence is weaker than the
enrichment literature claims.

We submit as a **Report** the manuscript "How widely a gene acts across the skeleton predicts how
widely its mutations cause disease," which shows that the convergence is real, that it is
anatomically local, and that a phenome-wide count is precisely the measurement that destroys it.

**The idea is simple enough to state in one sentence.** A phenome-wide comparison treats bone
density, serum urate and lung function as interchangeable units of pleiotropy and weighs them
against ontology terms drawn from every organ; if the correspondence is anatomical, matching
counts of unrelated things will average it away. So we matched the anatomy on both sides and
stayed inside one organ. Bone mineral density is available at six skeletal sites named after body
parts rather than machine settings, and the Human Phenotype Ontology names the same parts on the
disease side. Each gene then carries one number — at how many of the six sites does it act — and
that number predicts whether its mutations cause Mendelian skeletal disease, in three curations
that share no method of ascertainment, and how much of the body that disease involves.

**What closes the argument is a negative result we designed in advance to kill our own claim.**
The obvious objection is that breadth is just a proxy for how much a gene matters in general. If
so, breadth measured anywhere should predict phenotype extent anywhere. We rebuilt the entire
construction on seven subcortical brain volumes. Skeletal breadth predicts skeletal phenotype
extent; brain breadth predicts nothing, in either organ. The matrix is not symmetric, so breadth
is not general importance — and the reason the brain gives no answer is visible in the same data:
its measurement sites share too little genetic architecture for a shared layer to exist at all.
That is the published phenome-wide null seen from the inside. It converts an unexplained
non-result into a precondition that can be checked before an organ is studied, and it tells the
field where the rare–common correspondence can be found and where it will stay invisible.

**Why this belongs in AJHG rather than in a specialist bone journal.** The claim is about how
Mendelian and complex genetics relate to each other, tested in a place where the test is possible;
the skeleton is the instrument, not the subject. The same gene property spans two clinical ends
that are usually studied by separate communities: genes acting across the whole skeleton carry the
childhood dysplasias and, independently, the fracture risk of old age, while genes acting at a
single site carry neither. For variant interpretation this supplies a prior that costs nothing —
a gene of unknown significance acting across the whole skeleton is a better candidate for a
generalized dysplasia than one acting at a single site, and the number can be read off a table
that already exists.

**We have tried to leave reviewers less work, not more.** The finding replicates in GEFOS, which
shares no participants with UK Biobank, and survives entry alongside all six site-level statistics
from which breadth is derived. The limits are stated in the manuscript rather than left to be
discovered: breadth is a property of a gene and not a gene list, because redefining the layer in
the replication cohort yields lists that overlap by 19%; within the layer, fracture signal and
Mendelian disease are not associated with each other, so the defensible claim is the same layer
and not the same genes; the gene-level method has a p-value floor, and we report it. Every
threshold we chose is swept in full in the supplemental information rather than summarized.

All data are public. The code that reproduces every number and every figure is public with the
paper, and it carries a self-test that recomputes each numerical claim in the manuscript from the
table it came from and fails on any disagreement.
